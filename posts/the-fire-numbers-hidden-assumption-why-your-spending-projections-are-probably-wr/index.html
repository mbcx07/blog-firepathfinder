The FIRE Number's Hidden Assumption: Why Your Spending Projections Are Probably Wrong

2026-07-13 · FIRE Strategy · Tags: firepathfinder, The

When you first calculate your FIRE number, the math feels elegantly simple: multiply your annual expenses by 25 (or 30, or 33, depending on your risk tolerance) and you've got your target. That number becomes your north star, the figure you check every month as your savings grow. But here's the uncomfortable truth: that number is built on a foundation of assumptions that rarely hold up in the real world. Your spending projections are probably wrong—not because you're bad at math, but because life is messy, unpredictable, and full of expenses that don't fit neatly into a spreadsheet. In this article, we'll explore why your FIRE number might be misleading you, and more importantly, how to build a plan that can survive the curveballs life throws your way.

The Illusion of a Fixed Spending Number

Most FIRE calculators ask for your current annual expenses and then assume that number will remain constant (adjusted for inflation) throughout your retirement. It's a convenient simplification, but it's also a dangerous one. Your spending today is not a reliable predictor of your spending in retirement, especially if you're planning to retire early. Consider this: your current expenses likely include work-related costs—commuting, professional attire, lunches out, and maybe even a higher rent or mortgage payment because you live near a job hub. Once you retire, those costs may disappear, but new ones will emerge. You'll have more free time, which often means more spending on hobbies, travel, and social activities. You'll also face healthcare costs that aren't yet on your radar, especially if you're retiring before Medicare eligibility.

The truth is, your spending in retirement will be a moving target, influenced by your health, your interests, and the economy. If you base your FIRE number on a static figure, you're setting yourself up for either unnecessary frugality (if you overestimate) or a shortfall (if you underestimate). The key is to recognize that your spending projections are not a single number but a range of possibilities.

The Real-World Variables: Healthcare, Inflation, and Lifestyle Creep

Let's break down the three biggest variables that can derail your spending projections.

Healthcare: This is the elephant in the room for early retirees. If you're retiring at 45, you have 20 years before Medicare kicks in. During that time, you'll need to purchase health insurance on the open market or through a healthcare exchange. Premiums can be shockingly high, and out-of-pocket costs can escalate if you develop a chronic condition. A single major health event can wipe out years of careful planning. When you calculate your FIRE number, don't just plug in your current health insurance premium—research what a comparable plan would cost for your age and health status, and add a buffer for deductibles and copays.

Inflation: The 4% rule assumes a 2-3% inflation rate, but inflation isn't uniform. Healthcare costs have historically risen faster than general inflation, and education costs (if you have kids) are even worse. Your spending mix matters. If a large portion of your budget goes to categories with above-average inflation, your effective inflation rate will be higher than the national average. This is why it's crucial to look at your personal inflation rate, not just the CPI.

Lifestyle Creep: Even the most disciplined FIRE enthusiasts can fall victim to lifestyle creep after retirement. You might start with a modest travel budget, but then you discover a love for luxury cruises. Or you might decide to take up golf, which comes with equipment, club memberships, and green fees. The problem is that lifestyle creep is gradual—you don't notice it until your spending has crept up by 20% or more. And once you're retired, you don't have a salary to absorb that increase; it comes directly out of your portfolio.

Stress-Testing Your FIRE Plan: Scenario Planning

Instead of relying on a single spending number, create a range of scenarios. Start with your baseline—the minimum you need to cover essential expenses like housing, food, utilities, and insurance. Then add a comfortable scenario that includes discretionary spending for travel, dining, and hobbies. Finally, create a worst-case scenario that includes higher healthcare costs, a market downturn, and unexpected major expenses like a new roof or a car replacement.

Once you have these three numbers, calculate your FIRE number for each. Your actual FIRE number should be based on the comfortable scenario, but you should also have a plan for how you'd adjust if the worst-case scenario materializes. This isn't about being pessimistic; it's about being prepared. For example, if your portfolio drops by 30% in the first year of retirement, you might need to cut back on discretionary spending temporarily. Knowing that you can do that without sacrificing your basic needs gives you confidence and resilience.

Another useful exercise is to track your actual spending for a year before you retire. Not just your fixed bills, but every coffee, every impulse purchase, every subscription. You'll likely be surprised by where your money actually goes. Use that data to refine your projections, and then add a 10-15% buffer for the unknown.

Building Flexibility into Your Withdrawal Strategy

Your withdrawal rate is not set in stone. The 4% rule is a starting point, not a law. If you're willing to be flexible, you can make your portfolio last much longer. Consider a dynamic withdrawal strategy that adjusts based on market performance. For example, in years when your portfolio gains 10%, you might increase your withdrawal by 3% to keep up with inflation. In years when the market drops 20%, you might cut your withdrawal by 5% and rely on a cash buffer to cover essential expenses.

This approach, often called the "guardrails" method, allows you to enjoy your retirement without the constant fear of running out of money. It also gives you a built-in mechanism to respond to unexpected spending needs. If you have a year with a major medical expense, you can temporarily increase your withdrawal, knowing that you'll tighten the belt in future years if needed.

Another way to build flexibility is to have multiple income streams. A part-time consulting gig, a rental property, or a small online business can provide a cushion that reduces your reliance on your portfolio. Even a modest side income can make a huge difference in your long-term sustainability.

The Power of a Spending Buffer

One of the most effective ways to protect against spending projection errors is to build a dedicated buffer into your plan. This isn't the same as having an emergency fund—that's for pre-retirement. Instead, think of it as a "life happens" fund. Set aside a separate account with 1-2 years of living expenses in cash or low-risk investments. This fund is not part of your FIRE portfolio; it's a separate pool you can tap when unexpected expenses arise without having to sell investments at a loss.

For example, if your roof needs replacing and it costs $15,000, you can pull that from your buffer instead of selling stocks in a down market. This buffer also gives you psychological comfort. Knowing you have a safety net makes it easier to stick to your withdrawal plan during market volatility.

How much should you set aside? A good rule of thumb is to calculate your average annual unexpected expenses over the past five years—things like car repairs, medical deductibles, home maintenance—and multiply by two. If you haven't tracked that, start with 5% of your total portfolio as a buffer. It's a small price to pay for peace of mind.

Conclusion

Your FIRE number is not a magic formula; it's a living estimate that needs to be revisited and refined as your life evolves. The biggest mistake you can make is to treat it as a fixed target and then feel like a failure when reality doesn't match your projections. Instead, embrace the uncertainty. Build a plan that includes a range of spending scenarios, a flexible withdrawal strategy, and a dedicated buffer for the unexpected. By doing so, you'll not only be more financially secure, but you'll also be more emotionally prepared to enjoy the freedom that FIRE promises. After all, the goal isn't to hit a number—it's to live a life that's both fulfilling and sustainable. And that requires a plan that can bend without breaking.
