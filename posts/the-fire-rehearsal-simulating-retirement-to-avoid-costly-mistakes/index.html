The FIRE Rehearsal: Simulating Retirement to Avoid Costly Mistakes

2026-08-14 · Retirement · Tags: retirement, firepathfinder, The

You've crunched the numbers, optimized your savings rate, and visualized your last day of work countless times. But when the moment arrives to hand in your resignation, a nagging doubt creeps in: What if I'm missing something? The FIRE movement is built on meticulous planning, yet the biggest risk isn't your withdrawal rate or market volatility—it's the gap between your spreadsheet projections and the messy reality of a life without a paycheck. That's where the FIRE rehearsal comes in. Think of it as a dress rehearsal for your retirement: a structured, low-stakes experiment that lets you test your financial assumptions, your daily routines, and your emotional resilience before you make the leap. In this article, I'll show you how to design and execute a rehearsal that can save you from costly mistakes—both financial and psychological—and give you the confidence to retire early with eyes wide open.

Why a Rehearsal Is Your Secret Weapon

Most FIRE enthusiasts spend years optimizing their portfolio but almost no time optimizing their life after work. The result? A phenomenon I call the "retirement cliff": after the initial euphoria wears off, many early retirees struggle with boredom, loss of identity, and unexpected spending patterns. A rehearsal helps you identify these issues while you still have a job to fall back on. It also provides a reality check on your FIRE number. For instance, you might discover that your planned $40,000 annual spending doesn't account for the higher healthcare costs you'll face, or that you actually enjoy cooking at home more than dining out—saving you thousands. By simulating retirement in a controlled environment, you transform guesswork into data. You'll learn whether your passive income streams truly cover your lifestyle, and you'll develop the habits and routines that make early retirement fulfilling rather than aimless.

Designing Your Rehearsal: Time, Budget, and Boundaries

Before you start, decide on the scope of your rehearsal. A weekend-long "pretend retirement" is too short to reveal meaningful patterns; a full year might be impractical if you're still working. I recommend a minimum of three months, with six months being ideal. This gives you enough time to settle into new routines and experience seasonal variations in spending. Next, set clear boundaries. You'll be living on your planned retirement budget, but you'll still be working—so you need to separate the financial simulation from the time simulation. For example, you might continue working but redirect all your salary into savings and investment accounts, forcing yourself to live solely on your projected withdrawal amount. This is the "paycheck challenge." If you have a partner, involve them—retirement is a team sport, and you'll both need to buy into the experiment. Finally, define what you're testing. Are you checking if your budget is realistic? If you can fill your days meaningfully? If your relationship survives constant togetherness? Write down your hypotheses so you can evaluate them objectively at the end.

The Financial Simulation: Living on Your Withdrawal Rate

The core of any rehearsal is the financial stress test. Start by calculating your planned annual spending, then divide it by 12 to get a monthly target. For a more rigorous test, add a 10% buffer to account for surprises. During the rehearsal, track every single expense—use a budgeting app or a simple spreadsheet. This isn't about deprivation; it's about awareness. You'll likely discover that some categories are overfunded (maybe you spend less on gas because you're not commuting) and others are underfunded (like home maintenance or hobbies you've neglected). One of the biggest surprises for many is healthcare. If you're retiring before Medicare eligibility, your insurance premiums and out-of-pocket costs can be substantial. Simulate this by setting aside the exact amount you'd pay for an ACA plan, including copays and deductibles. Similarly, factor in taxes: if you're withdrawing from taxable accounts, you'll owe capital gains; if you're using a Roth ladder, you'll have conversion taxes. Use tax software to estimate your liability and subtract it from your budget. The goal is to live on your planned withdrawal rate, not a penny more. If you find yourself dipping into your savings or using credit cards, that's a red flag that your FIRE number needs adjustment.

The Psychological Simulation: Filling Your Days with Purpose

Money is only half the equation. The other half is how you'll spend your time—and your mental energy. During the rehearsal, you need to simulate the structure of retirement. That means waking up without an alarm, having no meetings to attend, and no boss to report to. For many, this is liberating; for others, it's terrifying. To make the most of your rehearsal, create a loose weekly schedule that includes activities you genuinely enjoy. This might include volunteering, learning a new skill, exercising, or pursuing a side passion. The key is to test whether these activities sustain your interest over months, not just days. I also recommend deliberately scheduling "boring" days—days with no plans at all—to see how you handle unstructured time. Do you feel anxious? Do you reach for your phone or the TV? These reactions reveal your tolerance for idleness, which is a crucial skill in retirement. Additionally, pay attention to your social life. If you derive most of your social interaction from colleagues, you'll need to build a new network. Join clubs, attend meetups, or take classes during your rehearsal to see what sticks. And don't forget the emotional side: retirement can trigger a loss of identity, especially if your job defined you. Use this time to explore who you are outside of your career. Journal about your feelings, and discuss them with your partner or a trusted friend.

The Soft Landing Approach: Gradual Transition Over Cold Turkey

If a full simulation feels too extreme, consider a softer version: the gradual transition. This isn't a rehearsal per se, but a phased approach that allows you to test retirement while still earning some income. For example, you could reduce your work hours to part-time for six months, living on your retirement budget while saving the rest. This is similar to Coast FI, but with a deliberate focus on lifestyle testing. Or you could take a sabbatical—many employers allow unpaid leave—and treat it as a mini-retirement. During this period, you'll experience the realities of no paycheck, but you'll have the security of knowing you can return to work if needed. The soft landing also helps you adjust psychologically. You can gradually shift your identity from "employee" to "retiree" without the shock of a hard stop. I've seen many FIRE aspirants use this approach to discover that they actually enjoy working part-time, or that they need more structure than they thought. The key is to treat it as a data-gathering exercise, not a vacation. Set specific goals, track your spending and time, and evaluate the results against your FIRE plan.

Learning from the Rehearsal: Adjusting Your FIRE Plan

Once your rehearsal ends, it's time to analyze the results. Look at your spending data: did you stay within budget? If not, where did you overspend? Was it a one-time event (like a car repair) or a recurring pattern (like dining out)? Adjust your FIRE number accordingly—if you consistently spent 10% more than planned, your portfolio needs to be 10% larger. But also look for positive surprises. Maybe you spent less on entertainment because you found free hobbies, or your grocery bill dropped because you have time to cook from scratch. These savings can lower your required withdrawal rate. On the psychological front, ask yourself: did I feel fulfilled? Did I miss work? Did I struggle with boredom? If you found the experience lonely or aimless, you might need to plan for more social activities or part-time work in retirement. You might also discover that you want to pursue a side business or freelance work—not for the money, but for the purpose. Use these insights to refine your retirement vision. Perhaps you'll decide to delay FIRE by a year to build a larger buffer, or you'll pivot to a barista FIRE approach where you work a few hours a week for benefits and social connection. The rehearsal is not a pass/fail test; it's a diagnostic tool that empowers you to make informed decisions.

Conclusion

The FIRE path is a marathon, not a sprint. You've already shown discipline in saving and investing, but the final mile—the transition from accumulation to withdrawal—deserves the same rigor. A FIRE rehearsal is the most effective way to de-risk that transition. By simulating your retirement finances, your daily routines, and your emotional well-being, you'll uncover hidden costs, develop resilience, and build the confidence to walk away from your job knowing you're truly ready. It's not about perfection; it's about preparation. So take the time to run your own rehearsal. Your future self—relaxed, purposeful, and financially secure—will thank you.
