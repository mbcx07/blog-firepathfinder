The FIRE Spending Smile: How to Plan for the Ups and Downs of Early Retirement Expenses

2026-06-15 · FIRE Strategy · Tags: firepathfinder, The

When we dream of Financial Independence, Retire Early (FIRE), we often picture a steady, predictable stream of withdrawals from our nest egg—a smooth, linear path from our last paycheck to our final days. But the reality of retirement spending is far more dynamic. In fact, most retirees experience what financial planners call the "spending smile": a U-shaped curve where expenses are high in the early years of retirement, dip in the middle, and then rise again later in life. For FIRE enthusiasts, understanding this pattern isn't just an academic exercise—it's the key to building a withdrawal strategy that actually works over a 40- or 50-year retirement horizon. Ignore the smile, and you risk either overspending in your go-go years or living too frugally when you could be enjoying the fruits of your labor. In this article, we'll dissect the spending smile, explain why it's especially relevant for early retirees, and offer practical strategies to align your withdrawal plan with the natural rhythm of your retirement.

The Three Phases of the Spending Smile

The spending smile is typically divided into three distinct phases, each with its own spending patterns and financial challenges. Understanding these phases helps you anticipate your future needs and avoid the common pitfall of assuming your expenses will remain constant.

Phase 1: The Go-Go Years (Ages 50–70 for most FIRE retirees)
In the first decade or two of early retirement, you're likely to be at your healthiest and most energetic. This is the time when you want to travel, take up expensive hobbies, dine out, and check off bucket-list items. Many FIRE retirees report spending 20–30% more in these early years than they did during their working lives, simply because they finally have the time and freedom to do all the things they've been postponing. This phase is also when you might face one-time expenses like home renovations, buying an RV, or helping your children with major life events. The go-go years are wonderful, but they demand a higher withdrawal rate—often 5% or more of your portfolio—to fund that burst of activity.

Phase 2: The Slow-Go Years (Ages 70–80)
As you age, your energy levels naturally decline, and your travel plans may shift from month-long treks to shorter, more comfortable getaways. You might find yourself spending more time at home, gardening, reading, or visiting family. Spending in this phase typically drops by 10–20% compared to the go-go years. You'll still have discretionary income, but the urgency to do everything at once fades. This is the sweet spot where your withdrawal rate can ease back to 3–4% of your portfolio, giving your investments a chance to recover from any early-retirement market downturns.

Phase 3: The No-Go Years (Ages 80+)
In the later stages of retirement, healthcare costs become the dominant driver of spending. Even with Medicare, out-of-pocket expenses for medications, in-home care, or assisted living can escalate quickly. This phase often sees spending rise again, sometimes to levels even higher than the go-go years, but for very different reasons. The no-go years are less about lifestyle and more about necessity. Planning for this phase means ensuring you have a buffer for medical emergencies and long-term care, which can easily eat through a portfolio if not anticipated.

Why the Spending Smile Is a FIRE Game-Changer

Traditional retirement planning often assumes a constant inflation-adjusted spending amount, which is the basis for the famous 4% rule. But that rule was designed for a 30-year retirement, not a 50-year one, and it doesn't account for the natural ebb and flow of expenses. For FIRE retirees, the spending smile has two critical implications.

First, if you lock in a fixed withdrawal rate based on your average expected spending, you'll likely be underfunded in the go-go years and overfunded in the slow-go years. That means you might miss out on the very experiences that made early retirement so appealing. Second, the sequence of returns risk—the danger of hitting a market downturn early in retirement—is amplified when your spending is highest. If you're withdrawing 5% of your portfolio during a bear market, you're selling more shares at depressed prices, which can permanently damage your portfolio's ability to recover. By acknowledging the spending smile, you can design a withdrawal strategy that is flexible enough to adapt to both your lifestyle and the markets.

Building a Flexible Withdrawal Strategy That Bends Without Breaking

So how do you translate the spending smile into a practical plan? The answer lies in dynamic withdrawal strategies, which adjust your spending based on your portfolio's performance. Here are three proven approaches that work well for FIRE retirees:

  • The Guardrails Approach (Guyton-Klinger): Start with a base withdrawal rate (e.g., 4.5% of your portfolio). Each year, adjust your withdrawal by inflation, but only if your portfolio's value hasn't dropped by more than 20% from its initial value. If it does, cut your withdrawal by 10%. Conversely, if your portfolio grows by more than 20% above the initial value, you can increase your withdrawal by 10%. This method allows you to spend more in good times and less in bad, naturally aligning with the spending smile.
  • The Percentage of Portfolio Method: Withdraw a fixed percentage (e.g., 4%) of your current portfolio balance each year. This automatically reduces spending in down markets and increases it in up markets. While it can lead to variable income, it's simple and ensures you never run out of money. To smooth out the volatility, many retirees combine this with a cash buffer.
  • The Bucket Strategy: Keep 1–2 years of living expenses in cash or short-term bonds, and the rest in a diversified portfolio. Each year, you refill the cash bucket by selling from your portfolio. During market downturns, you skip the refill and rely on the cash bucket, giving your investments time to recover. This approach provides a psychological safety net and prevents you from selling low.

Whichever method you choose, the key is to build in flexibility. Your withdrawal rate should be a range, not a fixed number. For example, you might plan to withdraw between 3% and 5% of your portfolio, depending on market conditions and your spending needs for that year.

Practical Tips for Managing the Spending Smile

Beyond the withdrawal strategy, there are several practical steps you can take to ensure your spending aligns with the smile without causing stress.

Create a Two-Tier Budget: Separate your essential expenses (housing, food, insurance, utilities) from your discretionary spending (travel, hobbies, entertainment). Your essential budget should be covered by a conservative withdrawal rate (e.g., 3% of your portfolio). Discretionary spending can be funded from a separate pool, such as a cash reserve or a side hustle, and can be adjusted up or down based on your portfolio's performance. This way, you never have to worry about cutting into your core lifestyle.

Plan for One-Time Splurges: The go-go years often include big-ticket items like a dream vacation or a new hobby setup. Instead of trying to fund these from your regular withdrawal, set aside a separate "fun fund" in the years leading up to retirement. For example, if you plan to spend $20,000 on a round-the-world trip in your first year, save that amount in a high-yield savings account before you retire. This takes the pressure off your portfolio and lets you enjoy the experience guilt-free.

Consider Part-Time Work or a Side Hustle: Many FIRE retirees find that a small income stream in the early years not only covers the higher spending but also provides a sense of purpose. Whether it's freelance consulting, teaching a class, or turning a hobby into a small business, earning even $10,000–$20,000 a year can significantly reduce the withdrawal rate during the go-go years. This is especially valuable if you retire during a market downturn, as it allows you to delay tapping your portfolio.

Revisit Your Plan Annually: The spending smile isn't a rigid schedule—it's a general trend. Your actual spending will vary based on your health, family circumstances, and personal preferences. Set aside time each year to review your actual expenses against your projections. Adjust your withdrawal rate and budget accordingly. This annual check-in ensures you're not drifting too far from your plan, and it gives you the confidence to increase spending when your portfolio is thriving.

The Psychological Shift: Giving Yourself Permission to Spend

For many FIRE enthusiasts, the hardest part of early retirement isn't the math—it's the mindset. After years of aggressive saving and frugal living, the idea of intentionally spending more can feel counterintuitive, even scary. You might find yourself hesitating to book that trip or upgrade your bike, worried that you're jeopardizing your future. This is where the spending smile can actually be a liberating framework. By acknowledging that your spending will naturally be higher in the early years, you give yourself permission to enjoy the fruits of your labor. You're not being reckless; you're following a well-researched pattern that accounts for the full arc of your retirement.

To ease the transition, start by tracking your spending for the first six months of retirement. You'll likely see that your actual expenses are in line with your plan, which builds confidence. Also, consider setting a "minimum spending" goal—a floor that you commit to spending each year, even if you don't feel like it. This ensures you don't become so frugal that you miss out on life. Remember, the goal of FIRE is not to hoard money until you die; it's to live a rich, fulfilling life on your own terms. The spending smile is a reminder that the early years are your golden opportunity to do just that.

Conclusion

The spending smile is not a curveball—it's a roadmap. By understanding that your retirement expenses will ebb and flow, you can design a withdrawal strategy that is both resilient and rewarding. Embrace the go-go years with a flexible plan that allows for higher spending, protect yourself with a cash buffer and dynamic withdrawal rules, and prepare for the no-go years by building in a healthcare cushion. Most importantly, give yourself the emotional permission to spend. You've worked hard to achieve FIRE; now it's time to let your money work for you in the way that brings the most joy. The spending smile isn't a warning—it's an invitation to live fully at every stage of your retirement.
