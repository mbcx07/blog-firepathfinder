The Retirement Spending Smile: How to Plan for the Ups and Downs of Early Retirement Expenses

2026-08-03 · Retirement · Tags: The, firepathfinder, retirement

When you spend years meticulously calculating your FIRE number, you probably assume that your annual expenses in retirement will remain as steady as a metronome. After all, the 4% rule is built on the premise of a consistent withdrawal rate. But real life—especially a retirement that could stretch across five or six decades—rarely follows a straight line. Instead, your spending is likely to trace a curve that looks like a smile: dipping in the early years, rising in the middle, and then tapering off again. Understanding this pattern is not just an academic exercise; it's the key to building a withdrawal strategy that actually works for your unique journey. In this article, we'll explore the retirement spending smile, why it matters for FIRE enthusiasts, and how you can prepare for the financial and emotional twists and turns without derailing your plans.

The Three Phases of Retirement Spending

Financial planners have long recognized that retirement spending isn't static. They often describe it in three phases: the go-go years, the slow-go years, and the no-go years. In the go-go phase, which typically covers the first decade or so of retirement, you're active, healthy, and eager to check off bucket-list items—travel, hobbies, dining out, and maybe even a second home. Spending tends to be higher than your pre-retirement baseline. Then comes the slow-go phase, where your energy and wanderlust begin to wane. You might still travel, but less frequently and more comfortably. Spending often levels off or even declines slightly. Finally, the no-go phase arrives, usually in your late 70s or 80s, when health issues limit your activities. At this point, spending on leisure drops, but medical costs may rise, creating a complex trade-off.

For early retirees, this traditional model needs a serious update. If you retire at 45, your go-go phase could last 20 years or more. You're not just taking a few years off to travel; you're redefining your entire life. The spending smile for a FIRE retiree is often deeper and wider because the timeline is so much longer. The initial dip might be more pronounced as you transition from a high-earning, high-spending career to a more frugal lifestyle. But then, as you settle into your new normal, you might find yourself spending more on experiences that truly bring you joy—not because you're inflating your lifestyle, but because you finally have the time to pursue them. The key is to recognize that your spending will not be a flat line, and to plan accordingly.

Why Early Retirees Face a Different Curve

Early retirees face a unique set of challenges that make the spending smile even more relevant. First, there's the sheer length of retirement. A 30-year retirement is the standard assumption in most calculators, but if you retire at 40, you could easily need your savings to last 50 years or more. That means you'll experience multiple economic cycles, including recessions, inflation spikes, and market downturns. Your spending needs will evolve not just with age, but with the broader economy. Second, healthcare is a wildcard. In the United States, early retirees often have to bridge the gap until Medicare kicks in at 65. That can mean paying for private insurance, which is notoriously expensive and unpredictable. As you age, your healthcare costs will likely rise, but the timing and magnitude are uncertain. Third, inflation compounds over decades. A 3% annual inflation rate means that $50,000 in today's dollars will be over $100,000 in 25 years. If your spending smile includes a mid-retirement surge, you need to account for that in your withdrawal plan.

Another factor is the psychological shift. Many FIRE enthusiasts are conditioned to save aggressively and spend frugally. When you suddenly have the freedom to spend without a paycheck, it can be jarring. Some people swing too far in the opposite direction, overspending in the early years and jeopardizing their long-term security. Others cling to their frugal habits so tightly that they never enjoy the fruits of their labor. The spending smile is a reminder that your spending will naturally fluctuate, and that's okay. The goal is not to keep your expenses artificially low forever, but to align your spending with your values and your stage of life.

Building a Flexible Withdrawal Strategy

So how do you translate the spending smile into a practical withdrawal plan? The traditional 4% rule assumes a constant withdrawal rate adjusted for inflation, but that's a one-size-fits-all approach that doesn't account for the natural ebb and flow of expenses. A better strategy is to use a variable withdrawal method that allows you to spend more in the early years, less in the middle, and then adjust as needed. One popular approach is the bucket strategy, where you divide your portfolio into three buckets: a cash bucket for near-term expenses (1-2 years), a bond bucket for medium-term needs (3-5 years), and a stock bucket for long-term growth. In years when the market is up, you replenish the cash bucket from your stock gains. In down years, you let the cash and bonds carry you through without selling stocks at a loss. This gives you the flexibility to spend more when you're healthy and active, and to pull back when the market is volatile.

Another approach is to set a base spending level that covers your essential needs—housing, food, utilities, insurance—and then have a discretionary spending budget that can flex up or down. For example, you might decide that your base spending is $40,000 per year, and you have an additional $10,000 to $20,000 for travel, hobbies, and other fun. In a good market year, you can spend the full discretionary amount. In a bad year, you can cut back to $5,000 without sacrificing your lifestyle. This not only protects your portfolio but also gives you permission to enjoy the go-go years without guilt. The key is to build this flexibility into your plan from the start, rather than trying to stick to a rigid budget that doesn't reflect reality.

Practical Tips for Managing the Spending Smile

Beyond the withdrawal strategy, there are several practical steps you can take to smooth out the spending smile and avoid financial stress. First, create a lumpy expense fund. Many retirees forget that big-ticket items like a new car, a roof replacement, or a medical emergency don't happen every year, but they will happen. By setting aside a separate fund for these irregular expenses, you can avoid dipping into your core portfolio or going into debt. Aim to save 1-2% of your portfolio value each year for these surprises. Second, consider a health savings account (HSA) if you're eligible. HSAs offer triple tax advantages—contributions are tax-deductible, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. For early retirees, an HSA can be a powerful tool to cover healthcare costs in the go-go years and beyond. Third, revisit your spending plan annually. The spending smile isn't a static curve; it's a living document. As your life changes—maybe you take up a new hobby, or you decide to move to a cheaper location—your spending will shift. Review your budget and your withdrawal rate at least once a year, and adjust as needed.

Another tip is to use the concept of "guilt-free spending" to your advantage. Many FIRE enthusiasts struggle with the transition from saving to spending. One way to overcome this is to allocate a specific percentage of your portfolio to "fun money" each year, and commit to spending it. For example, you might decide that 2% of your portfolio is for travel and experiences. If your portfolio is $1 million, that's $20,000 a year. Even if you don't spend it all, you give yourself permission to enjoy the fruits of your labor. This not only enhances your quality of life but also prevents the burnout that can come from extreme frugality. Remember, the goal of FIRE is not to die with the biggest pile of money; it's to live a fulfilling life on your own terms.

The Emotional Side of the Spending Smile

Finally, it's important to acknowledge the emotional component of the spending smile. When you first retire, you might feel a surge of freedom and excitement, which can lead to a spending spree. Then, as the novelty wears off, you might swing to the opposite extreme, worrying that you're spending too much and not saving enough. This emotional rollercoaster is normal, but it can be managed. One effective strategy is to set up a "spending check-in" with yourself or your partner. Every quarter, sit down and review your actual spending against your plan. Ask yourself: Are we spending on things that truly bring us joy? Are we cutting back in areas that don't matter? This reflection helps you stay aligned with your values, rather than reacting to fear or guilt. It also gives you a chance to celebrate your successes—like a year where you stayed within your budget while still having amazing experiences.

Another emotional challenge is the fear of running out of money. Even with a solid plan, that fear can linger. To combat it, consider using a Monte Carlo simulation or a retirement calculator that shows the probability of success under different spending scenarios. Seeing the numbers can give you confidence that your plan is robust, even if you have a few high-spending years. Additionally, remember that you have the ultimate safety net: your ability to earn income. Unlike a traditional retiree, you have decades of skills and experience. If the market tanks or your spending spikes, you can always pick up a part-time job, freelance, or start a side business. This flexibility is a huge advantage that many FIRE retirees overlook. It means you don't have to be as conservative as a 65-year-old who can't go back to work.

Conclusion

The retirement spending smile is not a warning; it's a roadmap. By understanding that your expenses will naturally rise and fall over the course of your early retirement, you can design a withdrawal strategy that is both flexible and resilient. You can give yourself permission to enjoy the go-go years without fear, while also building in safeguards for the slow-go and no-go phases. The key is to plan for variability, not against it. Build a lumpy expense fund, use a bucket approach, and revisit your spending plan regularly. Most importantly, remember that FIRE is not about living a life of deprivation—it's about living a life of intention. The spending smile is a reflection of that intention, a reminder that your money is a tool to support the life you want to live, at every stage. So embrace the curve, adjust your sails, and enjoy the journey.
